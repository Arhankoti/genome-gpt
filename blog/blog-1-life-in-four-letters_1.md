# Life Is Written in Four Letters

*Part 1 of a series on teaching an AI to read DNA. It starts with the simple question that wouldn't leave me alone.*

---

In biology class this year, we learned something that sounds almost too simple to be true. Every living thing, every redwood, every bacterium, you, me, is written in an alphabet of just four letters. A, C, G, T. About three billion of them packed into a single human cell.

I remember staring at the page and thinking something that definitely wasn't the point of the lesson.

*That's a text file. A really, really long text file.*

Around the same time, I'd been messing around with ChatGPT. Underneath all the magic, it's basically a program that's very good at one weirdly narrow task, which is predicting the next word in a sentence. Show it "the sky is" and it bets on "blue." Do that trick well enough, at a big enough scale, and somehow you get something that can write essays and explain jokes.

So two ideas ended up rattling around in my head at the same time. DNA is a language written in four letters. And we now have machines that learn languages by guessing what comes next. The thought that wouldn't leave me alone was this: if DNA is a language, could you build a language model that learns to read it?

I want to be upfront. I'm not a scientist. I'm a high school student with a laptop and, honestly, more curiosity than sense. But the strange and wonderful thing about right now is that you can actually try this. Before I get into any of the how, the models, the training, the stuff that fills up later posts, I want to just sit with the why. Because the why is the part that made me start instead of just daydream.

Three things grabbed me and won't let go.

## 1. Life is the most elegant code ever written

Four letters, in the right order, build everything that has ever been alive. Not describe everything. Build it. The instructions for a whale, a mushroom, and the bacteria in your gut are all written in the same four-symbol alphabet, just arranged differently.

And here's the part that gives me chills. This code has been running for about four billion years. Copying itself. Catching and fixing its own errors. Getting passed down, edited, and rerun across an unbroken chain of living things all the way to you reading this sentence.

As someone who likes computers, the idea that biology is information, that life is at some level something you could read and understand the way you read a program, is genuinely the coolest thing I have ever heard. We spend so much time treating "nature" and "technology" as opposites. But DNA is just code. Old, messy, brilliant code.

## 2. The letters actually matter

This isn't only beautiful. It's the difference between health and illness.

Sometimes a single letter in your DNA changes, a C where there should have been a T, and it means absolutely nothing. And sometimes that one swapped letter means a disease. A huge part of modern medicine is, at its core, one enormous question. Which changes matter, and which ones don't?

When you say it that way, something clicks. That's a pattern-recognition problem. You've got billions of letters, most changes are harmless noise, and buried in there are the few that aren't. Finding the meaningful needle in a haystack of "who cares" is exactly the kind of task AI has gotten strangely, almost spookily good at.

I'm not naive enough to think a high schooler is going to cure anything. But realizing that a real, life-and-death problem is shaped like a problem AI is good at made it impossible to look away.

## 3. You don't need permission anymore

This is the one that actually made me open my laptop instead of just thinking about it.

Genomes are free. You can download the complete genetic code of all kinds of organisms right now, for nothing, the way you'd download a PDF. The AI tools are open source, and a lot of them come with beginner tutorials written by people who want you to succeed. The same basic kind of model that powers chatbots can, with some tweaking, be pointed at DNA instead of English.

Think about what that means. A teenager in their bedroom now has access to tools that didn't exist for professional scientists fifteen years ago. Not worse versions. The actual tools. The gap between "curious kid" and "research lab" is smaller than it has ever been in history.

That felt like a thing I would genuinely regret not trying.

## So I decided to actually try

None of this was a plan to make a breakthrough. It was closer to an itch I couldn't stop scratching. DNA is a language, machines can learn languages, the tools are free, and nobody was going to stop me. At some point, daydreaming about it started to feel worse than just failing at it.

So I did. I decided to try to build a tiny AI that learns to read DNA, the same way ChatGPT learned to read English, one predicted symbol at a time.

That's where the next post picks up. How you actually take a few million letters and teach a "baby" language model to find the patterns hiding inside them. It gets humbling fast.

But that's Part 2. This one is just the invitation, the reason I think any of this is worth caring about at all.

If you take one thing from it, take this. We might be the first generation that gets to grow up treating biology as something you can read, and maybe one day write. That seems worth being curious about.
